Nine Kids, One Whale, and One Good Group of Adults

A Block Island beach day, a washed-up whale, and the quiet relief of parenting with people you trust.

At 9:20 in the morning, the plan changed.

We had been told the ferry was leaving at 10:30.

Then the message came through.

Sorry, it was actually leaving at 10.

That kind of update has a way of testing everything you thought you had figured out. Kids. Beach gear. Parking garage. Ferry tickets. A wagon. Enough adults and children moving in the same general direction to make the whole thing feel one snack delay away from becoming a logistical case study.

A few weeks ago, I wrote about how the weekend plan is not really the point.

This felt like the universe checking to see if I meant it.

Luckily, Cassie had packed everything the night before.

That part matters.

If she had not done that, the morning probably would have become the kind of scramble where everyone is moving, but nobody is actually ready. Instead, everything was already packed and grouped together. We still had to move fast, but we were not standing around wondering if we had towels, sunscreen, clothes, snacks, or whatever else a beach day with kids requires.

We were rushed.

But we were not lost.

There is a difference.

We pulled into the parking garage in New London with the kind of energy that felt less like parking and more like drifting a van into a spot. Everything got thrown together. Kids started moving. Adults started moving. I started moving faster than anyone expected a 260-pound dad to move.

For short periods.

Not distance.

Once I realized there were still other people boarding, and I heard one of the ferry workers say over the radio that they needed five more minutes for people still coming, the stress dropped enough for me to start being stupid.

Which is one of my more reliable parenting tools.

I sprinted past the kids in the bike lane just to mess with them. When we came to the railroad tracks, I started high-stepping over them like I was running drills instead of trying to catch a ferry to Block Island.

The kids were surprised I could still move that fast.

Our friends were already on the ferry.

They saw everything.

We found that out after we boarded and started telling them how we had to run. They just smiled and said they had watched the whole thing from the windows.

Including the high-stepping.

That came up several more times throughout the day.

I was not embarrassed. Not really. There are worse things than being seen by your friends acting like a fool to keep the kids laughing while you try to keep the day from falling apart.

The ferry ride over felt like part of the trip, not just transportation.

A group of us posted up on the top deck at the railing, kids and adults mixed together, wind blowing across the boat as the ferry pushed out toward Block Island.

Kylie started naming birds as they passed.

Not identifying them.

Naming them.

George. Fred. Wingsy.

At one point I pointed toward one and asked her what she had named that one, and she rattled it right off the top of her head like there was an official registry.

Before the fog reached us, she noticed it sitting off in the distance and asked why it looked weird over there. I told her it was probably rain or low clouds.

A little while later, we were in it.

The ferry horn started blowing about every minute. Most of the kids eventually went inside, but I liked it. I liked hearing the horn. I liked looking out for other boats.

There were people on the ferry dressed in pirate gear, maybe for some kind of Pirate Day on the island, and between that, the fog, and the horn, my brain went straight to old sailing ships.

And pirates.

Apparently, some parts of my brain are still eight years old.

When we got to Block Island, everyone disembarked and we had a quick meetup near the ferry.

The wagons were circled.

Literally.

It looked like some kind of beach-family wagon council. Adults checking gear. Kids waiting just long enough to ask how much longer. Everyone trying to figure out the next move.

That was when the whale officially entered the plan.

Someone on the ferry had shown us a post about a whale that had washed up near Town Beach. So before we started walking, the adults talked it through. Did we still go? Was the beach closed? Were we going to walk all the way there and find out the plan had fallen apart?

One of the dads said it would still be cool to see.

I agreed.

That settled it.

We were going to make the walk and see what happened.

The walk from town to the beach was not terrible.

It also was not the kind of walk where a parent can fully zone out and admire the ocean.

The shoulder narrowed in spots. Sand had pushed into parts of it. There was more traffic than I would have preferred. It was manageable, but it was still a “keep the kids where they belong and keep moving” kind of walk.

The group stretched out.

Kylie was farther ahead with one of the older girls and the lead group. Cassie and a few other adults were bringing up the rear with the wagons. I was somewhere in the middle, watching Kaysen and Corbin move along ahead of me.

That is when Kaysen reached over and took Corbin’s hand.

Nobody asked him to.

He just did it.

Kaysen was taking care of his little brother, keeping him beside him as we walked along the edge of the road. Corbin accepted it completely. He loved walking beside his best friend.

Kaysen even tried to switch places with him to put Corbin farther from the road.

Corbin objected.

Not because of the road.

Because the safer side had more sand, and Corbin did not like getting sand in his Crocs.

That is parenting in one scene.

One kid trying to protect his brother.

The other kid accepting the protection while filing a formal complaint about footwear conditions.

I do not want to make too much of it, because in the moment I was still doing parent math.

Road. Kids. Traffic. Sand. Group ahead. Adults behind. Keep moving.

But sometimes the thing worth noticing happens while you are busy managing everything else.

That was one of those things.

Eventually, the lead group reached the beach ahead of us and had already rented chairs and umbrellas by the time the rest of us arrived.

There had been a debate before the trip about whether the wagon should come at all. I was on Team Backpacks. Cassie was on Team Bring What We Need. To her credit, the way she packed everything probably saved the morning when the ferry time changed. To my credit, dragging a wagon through sand and along a narrow island road gave us some strong field data for next time.

Both things can be true.

Once we were set up, the whole day changed.

We had nine kids total.

Nine kids at the beach is not exactly relaxing in the traditional sense. It is more like managing a small, sandy, snack-powered village.

Somebody was always counting.

At one point, another parent and I were both apparently counting quietly to ourselves. Then we both said something like, “When did we get an extra kid?”

We had both counted ten.

Twice.

Turns out one of them was not ours.

That is what happens when kids start moving as a herd.

The lifeguards helped with the water side of things. That took a lot of pressure off the adults, but it did not eliminate the kid-drift problem. The kids would play in front of us for a while, then slowly migrate down the beach until one adult or another went to gather them back toward basecamp.

Still, once everyone was off the road and set up at the beach, the pressure dropped.

Not because the kids stopped needing us.

Because no one adult had to carry the whole thing alone.

The kids split into their own little groups.

Kylie and the older girls mostly claimed the water. Kaysen got buried in the sand by the middle crew and seemed perfectly happy about it. Corbin, Leighton, and the younger kids started catching sand fleas and laughing because they put one on Kaysen.

He laughed too.

Everyone was playing well.

That is not always how family outings go. Sometimes kids do better when there are more kids, not fewer. The extra chaos somehow creates more space. They split into little groups. They find someone new to play with. They stop treating their siblings like their only available entertainment.

The adults got to sit.

Actually sit.

I spent a good stretch of the day in a beach chair under an umbrella, listening and talking. Kids drifted through for snacks and drinks. At one point, kids were eating leftover french fries from another family, which is probably the clearest sign that we had stopped operating as separate households and had become one temporary beach village.

Nobody had to announce who was on snack duty.

Kids just passed through, and adults handed things out.

If a kid was starting to look red, one of the adults would call them over and tell them to put more sunscreen on.

It did not matter whose kid it was.

The kids listened well enough.

Sometimes they get a little sassy, but they know better than to sass Mr. Cody.

A few hundred yards down the beach, there was still a whale.

We were upwind, so it was not hanging over our spot. The water was also cold enough that only a few kids spent any meaningful amount of time swimming anyway. After we got settled, a few of the dads took whatever kids wanted to go see it.

It was smaller than I expected.

That sounds strange to say about a whale, but after hearing about it on the ferry and seeing videos people were passing around of a shark feeding on a whale carcass offshore, my brain had made it bigger somehow.

The boys were interested in it in the way boys tend to be interested in things that are dead, strange, large, gross, or possibly connected to sharks.

Kylie surprised me.

She asked to use my phone to take pictures of it.

Then she said she felt bad for the whale.

I told her that yes, it was sad. But I also explained that there is a circle of life, and the whale was not wasted. Other animals had already fed on it. More would. Even something sad can still become part of something else.

It was a quick dad answer in the moment.

But looking back, I am glad I did not just tell her there was no need to be sad.

There was a need to be sad.

At least for her.

She was looking at something huge and dead on the beach, and she felt something about it. My job was not to erase that feeling. My job was to stand there with her in it and give her enough understanding that the feeling had somewhere to go.

She listened.

Then she went back to enjoying the day.

That is how kids do it sometimes. They feel something, ask what they need to ask, and then return to the sand.

The whale did not take over the trip.

It became part of the story, then faded into the background.

Later, the kids listed “bodysurfing” as one of the best parts of the day.

I will give it to them. Technically, that is what they were doing.

But having grown up around the North Carolina coast, I had to put “bodysurfing” in quotation marks. These were tiny Block Island waves compared with the ones I remember from childhood.

Those waves were not tiny.

I remember getting knocked down, trying to find my feet, and getting hit by the next wave before I was fully standing again. There were times it felt like I nearly drowned. The ocean did not care that I was a kid. It just kept coming.

I do not always remember a lot from childhood easily. I often feel like the memories are there, but I cannot always build the bridge back to them. Every once in a while, a detail does it for me.

This time, it was the kids in the water.

And the kids on the sand.

The little waves brought back North Carolina. The sand brought back the system around those beach days: parents, aunts, uncles, grandparents, cousins, food, towels, someone watching, someone correcting, someone handing something out.

Our kids had a version of that on Block Island.

Not the same people.

A different kind of family.

But the feeling was familiar.

Kids running between adults. Someone noticing sunscreen. Someone handing out food. Someone counting. Someone redirecting. Someone stepping in without needing to ask whether it was their place.

On the walk back from seeing the whale, Corbin and Leighton ended up leading the pack and walked right past our camp. I was behind them, calling for Corbin, but he could not hear me over the beach.

Before I could catch up, another dad saw them coming and stepped out to head them off.

I would have gotten there.

But I did not have to.

That was the whole day in miniature.

Eventually, it was time to leave.

We loaded a van taxi with as many kids and wagon supplies as we could. The rest of us started the walk back toward the ferry. By then, the group had been split between the van and the walkers. Some kids were in one place. Some adults had already started back.

Somewhere behind me, near the van loading area, I heard an adult tell a kid:

“Just find one of the adults you know.”

I laughed to myself when I heard it.

That one sentence said more about the day than anything else could have.

Just find one of the adults you know.

On the walk.

At the beach.

Around the snacks.

With the sunscreen.

Counting to nine.

Accidentally counting to ten.

Taking kids to see a whale.

Letting them drift a little, but not too far.

There were enough adults watching that the kids did not need only their own parent every second. They just needed one of the known adults. One of the safe adults. One of the people who would hand them water, call them back, tell them to get sunscreen, or point them in the right direction.

When we got back near the ferry, we got ice cream before boarding.

That felt like the right ending to the island part of the day.

On the ferry home, most of the group ended up inside on the second level. The kids turned it into a traveling rec room. One table had kinetic sand. Another had Uno. A group of kids was in the corner on the floor playing War.

I sat up top.

Partly because I did not like being inside without being able to see the horizon while the ferry bounced around.

Mostly because it was peaceful.

The wind was cooler by then. The ocean mist came up over the boat. The sun slipped behind the clouds. After a full day in the sun, I wished I had brought a light long-sleeve shirt or a hoodie.

That is a note for next time.

Bring the hoodie.

From the top deck, kids came up every once in a while and hung out. I could see the sky, the water, and the end of the day stretching out behind us.

The trip had started with a changed ferry time, a parking garage sprint, and me high-stepping railroad tracks while my friends watched through ferry windows.

It became fog horns, named birds, wagons circled at Old Harbor, a narrow walk to the beach, Kaysen holding Corbin’s hand, nine kids becoming ten for a second, cold water, sand fleas, sunscreen, french fries from other families, a washed-up whale, a quiet conversation with Kylie, ice cream, and a ferry ride home.

We went to Block Island for a beach day.

We got one.

But we also got one of those days that reminds you why it matters who you go with.

Because sometimes the best part of parenting is not that the work disappears.

It is realizing you are not the only one watching.
