How My Son Taught Me to Make an Acorn Whistle

By Cody Spencer

A walk with my boys became an unexpected lesson in making an acorn-cap whistle—and in who gets to be the teacher.

I was out walking with Kaysen and Corbin when Kaysen picked up an acorn, held it between his hands and made a whistle.

I'm sorry, you did what?

How does my son know how to do this and I don't?

I joked that he was going to be the subject of the next River Bend Outdoors article. He immediately thought he was going to be famous. I didn't have the heart to tell him that his mom probably accounts for a significant percentage of our readership.

He took it seriously, though. He wanted me to interview him about how he learned it, and he wanted pictures of him demonstrating how to do it. So here we are.

From the playground to the woods

A few of Kaysen's friends at school had been trying to whistle with acorns on the playground. He decided to give it a shot because he thought it might be useful if he ever got lost in the woods.

When I asked how it would help, his answer was pretty straightforward: people could hear it.

He managed to get a little whistle out of one when he first tried. Over the past month, he's practiced here and there and can now get a surprisingly good sound out of it. Even during the roughly 20 minutes I watched him experimenting on our walk, I could see him getting the hang of it.

According to Kaysen, the hardest part is figuring out how to hold the acorn. His advice is to keep trying and find a size that works for you. He preferred a smaller one. I was working with a pretty big one, which might have something to do with the difference in the size of our hands.

How to make an acorn whistle

The part you actually need is the cap of the acorn, not the nut itself. There's nothing to carve, assemble or buy.

Start with the hollow cap from an acorn.

  1. Find a clean, intact acorn cap. Try a few different sizes.
  2. Hold the cap between your thumbs and index fingers, with the hollow side facing you.
  3. Bring the thumbs together so they cover most of the cap. Angle the tips slightly apart to leave a small triangular opening at the top.
  4. Bring your thumb knuckles to your lips and blow firmly across the little opening, directing the air toward the exposed rim of the cap.
  5. If all you get is air, adjust your thumb position, the angle of the cap or the way you're blowing. Small changes can make a big difference.

The grip is the tricky part. The small opening at the top is what you are trying to create; this close-up shows how the thumbs come together.

Kaysen's biggest piece of advice was probably the most useful: don't give up after the first attempt. I watched a kid take something off the ground and make a surprisingly piercing whistle with a little practice.

For another demonstration and a more detailed explanation of the technique, see ScienceToyMaker's acorn-cap whistle instructions.

Could this actually be useful outdoors?

Kaysen's reason for learning the trick was what made me want to look into it a little further.

It turns out this isn't just something a few kids came up with on a playground. The International Oak Society has written about acorn-cap whistling, including a tradition of Spanish goatherds using the caps to make sounds. It also mentions the technique as a possible way to call for help in the woods.

And Kaysen was right about why a whistle can be useful. The National Park Service's outdoor emergency guide recommends carrying a whistle because signaling with one helps people find you without wearing yourself out yelling. Its Rocky Mountain National Park search-and-rescue guidance explains that searchers may call out or use whistles themselves, listening for a response from the missing person.

That's a good reason to teach kids to stay in a safe spot if they're separated and respond to people calling for them. The NPS also reminds children that the people looking for them aren't angry; they want to bring them home.

Would I leave a dedicated emergency whistle at home because I know how to make one out of an acorn? No. An actual safety whistle is easier to use, doesn't depend on finding a suitable cap, and is the one I'd want in my pocket. I didn't measure the range or volume of the acorn whistle, so I'm not making claims about how far it could be heard. But knowing how to improvise one is another option if you don't have your usual equipment.

The teacher becomes the student

Kaysen was excited that he got to teach me something. He also made a very determined attempt to teach Corbin.

If you've ever tried to explain the grip and blowing technique to a four-year-old, you can probably imagine how long that lesson lasted.

What I liked about watching him was that a month ago he was the one trying to learn something from his friends. Now he was the one trying to pass it along.

When I asked him what his favorite part of the walk had been, it wasn't the whistle or his impending internet fame. It was seeing the deer prints and walking with his family.

Knowledge weighs nothing

We spend a lot of time talking about what equipment we should bring into the outdoors. Equipment matters, and I'm not suggesting we leave it behind. But we should also spend a little time thinking about what we know how to do.

Knowing how to use a map, recognize a landmark or make an improvised signal gives us options. Learning those skills doesn't always require buying something or setting aside an entire day. Sometimes it just takes picking up an acorn and trying something new.

Unlike equipment, knowledge weighs nothing.

On that walk I learned something new from my son. Maybe the next time you're out walking, you'll pick up an acorn cap and give it a try yourself.

Kaysen, you earned your article.
