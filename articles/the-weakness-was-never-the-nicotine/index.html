I Thought Hiding It Was Protecting Us

An honest reflection on hiding a habit, trust at home, and learning to be more open.

A few days ago, before school, the kids were playing outside waiting for the bus.

Corbin, my youngest was sitting in the passenger seat of my car.

My wife walked over to him, no big deal.

Then I look back at her as she held up a can of nicotine pouches she found in my car.

She didn’t yell

Didn’t start an argument.

She just held them up…

Honestly, that was worse.

Because we’ve been here before.

I quit.

Then I “mostly quit.”

Then I quietly convince myself one can isn’t a big deal.

Then somehow, I end up surprised when the same problem shows back up.

Not exactly elite-level decision making.

The thing is, nicotine has had a grip on me for a long time. Since around 2006.

Somewhere between military life, stress, routines, and just surviving hard seasons, my brain attached nicotine to relief.

Life gets loud

Pressure builds

Nicotine became the little switch that made everything feel manageable for a few minutes.

Or at least manageable enough.

Every time I bought another can, I told myself the same thing:

This is the last one.

But if you’ve ever struggled with anything, you probably know how that story usually goes.

What hit me that morning wasn’t really about the nicotine though.

It was the hiding.

Over the last few days, I’ve thought about that moment a lot.

For a long time, I convinced myself hiding it was actually protecting both of us.

If my wife didn’t know, she wouldn’t have to carry the stress.

If I could just quietly fix it myself, then nobody would get hurt.

No disappointment.

No conflict.

No hard conversations.

Problem solved.

Except… not really.

Because when I slow down and actually look at it honestly

Hiding it didn’t protect either of us, it protected my image.

That realization stung a little.

Okay, more than a little.

Because while I thought I was managing a private struggle, what I was actually doing was creating distance.

She wasn’t just frustrated about nicotine.

She was lonely.

And that part hit me harder than getting caught.

Because this woman has stood beside me through deployments, stress, career changes, parenting chaos, and every version of me in between.

Meanwhile, I’m over here trying to quietly manage my own struggle like some secret side mission nobody else can know about.

Turns out marriage doesn’t work great when one person is quietly fighting battles the other person never even knew existed.

Who knew? (Probably my wife.)

The harder truth is that every other time this happened, I thought “being better” meant quitting.

  • White-knuckle it.
  • Get rid of the evidence.
  • Handle it quietly.
  • Try harder.

But after sitting with this for a few days, something actually clicked.

Being better doesn’t mean magically becoming perfect overnight.

It means being honest.

Honest about the struggle.

Honest about how it affects the people around me.

Honest enough to admit that maybe the real problem isn’t weakness — it’s pride.

Because hiding things sounds protective right up until you realize the silence is hurting both people.

And honestly, this whole thing keeps bringing me back to the three ideas behind River Bend Outdoors:

Slow Down.

Be Present.

Live Intentionally.

Slow down enough to actually look at what’s happening instead of rushing past uncomfortable truths.

Be present enough to recognize how your actions affect the people closest to you.

Live intentionally enough to stop hoping problems quietly disappear on their own.

I don’t have some perfect ending to this.

No dramatic promise.

No big speech about how everything changes starting today.

Just an uncomfortable realization that landed differently this time.

I don’t think my wife needs another promise.

I think she needs honesty.

Consistency.

Transparency.

And probably fewer surprises hidden in my car.

So that’s the next step.

Not perfection.

Just showing up more honestly than I did yesterday.
