The Rep That Still Counted

An imperfect attempt at being a more present husband.

Last night, Cassie and I were sitting outside a Mexican restaurant next to Kylie’s dance studio, enjoying one of those rare little gaps in the day where nobody was actively late, bleeding, crying, or asking for a snack we did not bring.

It was a beautiful afternoon.

Corbin was blowing bubbles beside our table while Kaysen tried to pop them before they hit the ground.

Kylie was at dance. The boys were laughing. Cassie had work soon. We had just enough time to eat dinner, sit for a little while, and pretend for a few minutes that the family schedule was not being held together with duct tape, caffeine, and whoever remembered where the dance bag was.

From the outside, it probably looked like a peaceful family moment.

And in a lot of ways, it was.

But sitting across from me, Cassie was carrying more than anyone walking by would have known.

She started talking about how overwhelmed she felt. Not in a dramatic way. Not in a “drop everything and fix this” kind of way. More like she was finally letting a little bit of the weight out before she had to head to work and keep going.

At first, I listened.

That part is important, because I do not want to rewrite the story like I immediately failed the whole thing. I was trying. I was paying attention. I wanted to understand what she was saying.

Then she shared something that hurt her.

And there it was.

The old instinct.

I jumped in with a reason she should not have to feel that way.

I did not mean it badly. I was not trying to dismiss her. I was trying to protect her from feeling sad because I know what kind of wife, mom, and person she is. When someone you love is hurting, especially when you believe they do not deserve to feel that way, it is hard not to reach for the fastest tool you have.

For me, that tool is usually an explanation.

  • Here is why this makes sense.
  • Here is why you do not need to carry it that way.
  • Here is what is probably happening.
  • Here is the reason this should hurt less.

The problem is that explanations can be true and still not be what the person needs first.

Sometimes the first need is not a solution.

Sometimes the first need is to be heard.

I think I caught myself partway through. I remember trying to back off and return to listening. But by then the conversation had already started to wind down.

Then life moved on, because that is what life with kids does.

Cassie loaded the boys into my car while I paid the bill. She went to work. I took the kids home for the night. There were probably baths, pajamas, snacks, bedtime negotiations, and at least one child who suddenly remembered something extremely important at the exact moment they were supposed to be asleep.

The moment passed.

Normally, I probably would have liked that.

A hard conversation ended quietly. Nobody was mad. Nobody was arguing. The evening kept moving.

That used to feel like a win.

But later, when I slowed down enough to actually think about it, I started wondering if the conversation ending quietly was not the same thing as connection.

  • Maybe she had shared what she needed to share.
  • Maybe I had listened enough.
  • Or maybe I left her hanging a little bit.

That is the uncomfortable part.

Not because I did something terrible. This was not some catastrophic husband failure. It was a normal, ordinary, imperfect marriage moment.

But those are the ones I am trying to notice now.

That is a big part of why I write these things down.

There is always something else to do. Always.

A kid needs a bath. Someone needs a snack. There is work tomorrow. The grass needs to be cut. The bill needs to be paid. The dance bag needs to be found. The youngest is probably holding a rock he considers essential to national security.

Life does not naturally create much space for reflection.

So, I am trying to create it on purpose.

Writing gives me a way to slow the moment down after it is already over. It gives me a chance to ask what I missed, what I felt, what I did well, and where I want to do better next time.

It does not fix the moment I missed.

But it gives me a rep.

And I am starting to believe the rep’s matter.

A rep is not perfection. It is not getting every conversation right. It is not suddenly becoming the calm, emotionally open, fully present husband and father I want to be overnight.

  • A rep is the work that moves me in that direction.
  • It is noticing that I tried to listen.
  • It is admitting that I slipped into fixing.
  • It is recognizing that I wanted to protect Cassie from sadness, but may have rushed past the part where she needed me to simply stay with her in it.
  • It is being honest enough to say, “That was messy, but it was a rep nonetheless.”

But, enough reps change the baseline.

That is the part I keep coming back to.

The goal is not one perfect moment. The goal is to keep putting in the work until the better response becomes more natural than the old one.

  • Listen longer.
  • Fix slower.
  • Stay present.
  • Notice sooner.
  • Repair faster.

That is the husband I want to be.

That is the father I want to be.

And if writing about a quiet dinner outside a Mexican restaurant helps me get one rep closer to that, then the rep counted.

I am curious if anyone else has had one of those moments where you realized later that you may have tried to fix when someone just needed you to listen. How did you learn to catch it sooner?
