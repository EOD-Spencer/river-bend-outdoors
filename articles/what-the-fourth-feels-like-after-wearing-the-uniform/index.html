What the Fourth Feels Like After Wearing the Uniform

How fireworks, fatherhood, storms, and a tired kid asleep on the couch reminded me what freedom is really supposed to protect.

The Fourth had already gotten hold of the day before anyone even got wet.

There were patriotic stickers, bare feet, sunscreen, towels, and that pre-party energy kids get when they know there is water nearby, food somewhere close, and a whole yard full of other kids to run around with.

At the time, it looked like the start of a summer party.

By the next evening, Corbin would be asleep on the couch while I cooked mac and cheese, still wearing little patriotic stars on his face from the day before.

It may have been July 5th by then, but the Fourth was still written all over him.

He was clean, finally in pajamas, wrapped up on the couch, completely done after two days of water, heat, friends, storms, swimming, and kid-level chaos.

Cassie had handled baths and finally got to sit down for a minute.

The house was settling.

Dinner was on the stove.

And I looked at him and wondered what parts of the weekend would stay with him.

  • The giant water slide.
  • Running around with other kids.
  • The storm.
  • The lanterns in the living room when the power was out.
  • The pool the next day.
  • The feeling of being surrounded by family, friends, and summer.

I do not know what he will remember clearly.

But I know what I remember from being a kid around his age.

  • Fireworks.
  • Cookouts.
  • Summer heat.
  • And trying not to blow our fingers off with fireworks my dad brought back from South of the Border.

Not the little fireworks either.

I mean the good ones.

The kind that were apparently illegal in North Carolina, which is why I was never allowed to ride with him when he went to get them. At the time, I probably thought that was unfair. Looking back, I understand the logic.

He did not want his kid sitting in the truck if he got busted hauling a small family fireworks operation across state lines.

But those mortars were incredible.

They were massive. At least they felt massive to me then. The kind of fireworks that made every kid stop what they were doing and every adult pretend they were not slightly concerned.

They would launch into the air, hang there for a second, and then explode with that deep thump you could feel in your chest.

That was the Fourth to me as a kid.

  • Cookouts.
  • Smoke.
  • Noise.
  • A little bit of danger.
  • A little bit of secrecy.
  • And the belief that the best fireworks were the ones that made your ribs feel like they had been tapped from the inside.

Then I joined the military.

You might think wearing the uniform would have made the Fourth more serious. More reflective. More polished and patriotic.

That is not exactly how it went.

For a while, the Fourth became a huge party with a bunch of Explosive Ordnance Disposal (EOD) technicians, plenty of alcohol, and enough fireworks that the shed we kept them in for the night probably should have been placarded.

EOD techs and recreational fireworks was probably not a combination that looked great on a risk assessment.

But at the time, it made sense to us.

We were young.

We were trained.

We worked around things that went boom for a living.

And like a lot of young men in dangerous jobs, we believed we were invincible.

Not because anyone said it out loud.

It was just in the air.

In the jokes.

In the stories.

In the way everyone stood a little too close.

In the way we trusted each other.

In the way danger had become normal enough that a ridiculous pile of fireworks felt less like a warning sign and more like a proper holiday.

I am not saying that was smart.

I am saying that was real.

And for a while, that was what the Fourth felt like after putting on the uniform.

  • Louder.
  • Bigger.
  • More reckless.
  • More confident.
  • Still full of thump.

But somewhere along the way, that changed.

I do not think it happened all at once. I do not remember a single moment where I woke up and stopped feeling invincible.

But if I had to name the shift, I would say it happened when I became a dad.

Fatherhood has a way of changing the volume on things.

The same fireworks are still loud, but they are no longer the center of the night. The cookout still matters, but not just because of the food. The holiday is still fun, but now there are kids running through the yard, someone asking where the paper plates are, someone else holding a juice box, and at least one child with ketchup on their mouth, hands, and probably shirt.

That is the scene I think about now.

And this year, I actually got to see it.

The kids did not need much convincing once they saw the water slide.

A giant inflatable water slide took over the yard.

And honestly, I think the adults may have enjoyed it more than the kids did.

At one point, several dads were sitting at the bottom of the slide discussing the flow rate of the soaker hose at the top. Apparently, one side of the slide was slightly slower than the other, and that could not stand.

Before I knew it, one dad was checking hose pressure between hoses like we were diagnosing a municipal water issue, while another was climbing up the inflatable slide, trying to hold onto the hose and balance near the top so he could “fix” the problem.

This was not an emergency.

It was barely even a problem.

But to a group of dads standing around a water slide on the Fourth of July, it had become a matter of performance, fairness, and basic American standards.

The kids ran barefoot through the grass.

Foam stuck to their arms, legs, hair, and probably places we will find later.

Kaysen walked around with a megaphone, because apparently freedom also includes public announcements from children who should not have public announcement authority.

Parents sat under tents and umbrellas with that half-relaxed, half-alert look all parents understand.

Friends stepped in naturally.

Someone watching the little ones.

Someone grabbing food.

Someone laughing.

Someone asking, “Where is your other shoe?”

Someone making sure the kids were having the kind of summer evening they would remember.

That kind of evening feels different to me now.

It feels peaceful.

Not quiet, exactly. Kids, fireworks, inflatable slides, and megaphones do not usually create quiet.

But peaceful in the deeper sense.

Peaceful because everyone is there.

Because nobody has to carry the whole night alone.

Because the kids feel safe enough to run around sticky, soaked, loud, and happy.

Because the adults get to sit together and enjoy it.

One family brought the tents and tables. Other families brought food, drinks, kids, towels, patience, and whatever else the day needed. Everyone pitched in somewhere. Everyone helped make it what it was.

That is the part I notice more now.

Not just the flag.

Not just the fireworks.

Not just the big patriotic idea of the day.

The people.

The families.

The way everyone brings something, watches someone, helps somewhere, and somehow turns a yard full of chaos into a good summer evening.

Then the storm stopped being background weather and became the plan.

At first, it was just rain. The kind of summer rain that sends adults under cover and makes kids somehow more committed to being outside.

Then it got stronger.

A few of us were trying to keep a folding canopy from blowing away until lightning struck close enough to make the decision for us.

The canopy was no longer the priority.

Getting everyone inside was.

We moved into the house, and by then every kid was feeding off every other kid. Someone was crying. Then another kid was crying. Then the whole thing turned into a scared-kid chain reaction.

That is when the night shifted from party mode to parent mode.

It was time to come up with a plan quick and execute it.

The kids got staged near the garage while the dads pulled cars up close. Then it was my turn, so I ran down to the car, backed it up near the garage, and everyone piled in.

  • Wet kids.
  • Scared kids.
  • Crying kids.
  • Kids wearing someone else’s pajamas.
  • Kids who could not find their shoes.
  • Kids convinced the storm was going to ruin them personally.

I was not scared of the storm.

I have seen worse afternoon showers living in the South. This was a strong storm, and we had to respect it, especially once the lightning got close, but we were not out of options.

Worst case, we would have turned around, gone back to our friends’ house, and stayed the night.

The kids did not need a weather report, though.

They needed to know the adults had it handled.

So we loaded up and headed home.

Then we ran into trees down across the road.

So we turned around, went the long way, and hoped there was not another tree down that way too.

At that point, it was not dramatic. It was not cinematic. It was just parenting.

Get everyone home.

Keep everyone calm.

Solve the next problem.

Then solve the one after that.

When we finally pulled into our driveway, there were no lights on.

No power.

Which meant no air conditioning.

And my first thought was basically, “Great. Now the kids are going to freak out about the power being out.”

So everyone piled into the living room.

  • Couch.
  • Floor.
  • Blankets.
  • Battery-powered lanterns.
  • Hot, sticky air that was not moving anywhere.

It was not the clean, easy ending to the Fourth.

It was tired kids after a full day of sun, water, food, friends, storms, crying, downed trees, and a long ride home.

We kept telling them the same thing:

As long as we are together and we are safe, nothing else matters.

And that was true.

The power being out did not matter.

The air conditioning being out did not matter.

The missing shoes, wrong pajamas, and ruined plans did not matter.

Everyone was home.

Everyone was safe.

Everyone was together.

Eventually, the kids fell asleep because they were exhausted from the heat and from playing all day.

Nobody slept great.

By the next day, the Fourth had turned into the fifth, but it still felt connected to the same story.

The house was hot. The power had been out. The kids were tired and cranky from a full day of playing, a storm, a late night, and sleeping wherever they landed in the living room.

So we did the next part.

We went back over to help clean up from the party, because the night before everyone had to leave fast.

That is part of it too.

The good people are not just the people you celebrate with when the weather is nice.

They are the people who bring tents and tables.

The people who help set up.

The people who bring food.

The people who keep an eye on each other’s kids.

The people who jump in without needing a formal assignment.

The people who help make the weekend feel easy, even when there is a lot of work happening behind the scenes.

And it was not just the party group.

Back near our road, neighbors were out with tractors and chainsaws like an honorary town debris removal task force, clearing trees so people could get through.

It was one of those small-town things that feels ordinary and impressive at the same time.

Somebody has a tractor.

Somebody has a saw.

Somebody knows where the tree needs to go.

And suddenly the road is passable enough for tired families to keep moving through the day.

There was another part I kept thinking about too.

Even if we could not get home the night before, we had places we could go.

That is not a small thing.

We had friends who would have taken us back in. We had neighbors we could call. If I had really needed it, I know someone nearby would have fired up a chainsaw, gotten on a tractor, and helped cut a path home.

That kind of thing is easy to take for granted until a storm knocks trees across the road and reminds you how much it matters to live around people who still show up for each other.

And I felt grateful for that too.

Grateful for the town we live in.

Grateful for the neighbors.

Grateful that our kids are growing up in a place where outdoor adventures are not some rare vacation thing, but part of the rhythm of life here.

  • The beach.
  • The woods.
  • The pool.
  • The trails.
  • The water.
  • The backyards.
  • The people.

All of it.

Later, we met back up at the pool to beat the heat.

The kids swam again, because apparently one full day of water and chaos is not enough when you are a kid in July.

The adults floated at the edge of the pool looking exactly like people who had survived a holiday weekend, a storm, a power outage, cranky kids, cleanup, and one more round of swimming.

Then we came home.

  • Showers.
  • Pajamas.
  • Mac and cheese.
  • Laundry (so much laundry)

Cassie handled baths and finally got to sit down on the couch for a minute. Corbin still had the little patriotic stars on his face from the day before. He curled up under a blanket and fell asleep on the couch while I cooked dinner.

That felt like the real end of the weekend.

Not fireworks.

Not a clean finale.

Just a tired kid with face stickers still on, a hot house slowly settling down, dinner on the stove, and all of us home.

And standing there, tired from the whole weekend, I just felt glad.

Glad we had the weekend.

Glad we had good people around us.

Glad our kids get to grow up around families who show up, pitch in, share the load, ride out storms, clean up the next day, and meet back at the pool like that is just what you do.

After wearing the uniform, the Fourth does not feel less meaningful to me.

It feels more meaningful.

Not always in a dramatic way. Not in a way that needs a speech or a perfect flag photo or a long explanation.

It is more meaningful because I understand a little more now.

I understand that independence is not just a word we say because it belongs on a calendar.

I understand that service puts weight on families.

It means missing things.

It means leaving when you do not get to choose the timing.

It means disappearing from home for reasons that are not always easy to explain and for lengths of time that are not always easy to accept.

It means the people who love you have to carry parts of life without you, even when they did not sign the same paperwork you did.

That is part of what I feel on the Fourth now.

Pride, yes.

I am proud to have served.

I am proud of the work. I am proud of the people I served with. I am proud to understand, even in my own small way, that “freedom is not free” is not just a slogan.

It is costly in ways that show up around kitchen tables, in missed birthdays, in hard phone calls, in strong spouses, in kids growing and changing while someone is gone.

But I also feel gratitude.

Gratitude for the life we have been able to build.

Gratitude for a wife who has stood beside me from day one.

Gratitude for the family we have built together.

Gratitude for the path that brought us here, even when parts of that path were hard.

And most of all, gratitude that I am still learning how to be present enough to notice it.

That might be the biggest change for me.

As a kid, the Fourth was about the explosion.

As a young EOD tech, it was about the party, the brotherhood, the confidence, and the feeling that nothing could touch us.

Now, as a husband and father, the Fourth feels more like a reminder to look around.

To notice the storm that changed the plan.

To notice the scared kids being reminded they were safe.

To notice the neighbors clearing the road.

To notice the pool water the next day.

To notice the pajamas.

To notice the mac and cheese.

To notice the kid asleep on the couch.

To notice the life we have been able to build.

Freedom is not only something defended somewhere far away.

It is also something lived right here.

In the backyard.

At the cookout.

Under the fireworks.

During the storm.

On the long way home.

In the dark living room.

At the pool the next day.

With kids laughing.

With families choosing each other.

With friends showing up.

With neighbors clearing the road.

With another summer weekend we are lucky enough to have.

That is the part I do not want to miss anymore.

Because independence is not just something a country declares.

It is the freedom to build a life.

To choose a path.

To raise a family.

To gather with people we love.

To keep growing.

To keep healing.

To keep learning how to be present for the ordinary moments that were never really ordinary at all.

I still love the fireworks.

I still love the cookouts.

I still love the thump of a good mortar in the distance.

But after wearing the uniform, the Fourth feels different.

It feels less like proving we are invincible.

And more like being grateful that we do not have to be.
