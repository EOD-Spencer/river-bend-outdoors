The Dockside Classic

A tense battle between brothers

Ladies and gentlemen, welcome to the Dockside Classic.

The conditions were perfect.

The water was calm.

The stakes were unnecessarily high.

The competitors were small, emotional, and operating under a scoring system that had not been approved by any governing body.

The day opened with Kaysen making a strong statement from a giant rock near the shore.

First cast? Not quite.

Clean execution? Debatable.

But did he bring the first fish of the day to hand?

He did.

It was not a giant.

It was not going on the wall.

But in this tournament, size was not the story. Timing was the story.

And with that first catch, Kaysen jumped out to an early 1-0 lead.

Cassie, watching from the paddle board division, immediately attempted to return to shore for the celebration. Unfortunately, she was working against both distance and the speed of a child’s attention span. By the time she made it back, the fish had been admired, photographed, emotionally processed, and released back into the lake.

That is just how fast things move at this level.

A short time later, the field relocated to the dock.

That is when the tournament changed.

Corbin stepped up with the kind of confidence you usually see from someone who does not fully understand hooks yet.

The cast went out.

The line tightened.

And there it was.

A fish.

Not just a fish.

His first fish.

Now, there is some controversy here. Kaysen did provide reel-in assistance, and he has been very clear that this should be considered when the final results are reviewed.

His position is simple: if he reeled it in, he should receive credit.

Possibly full credit.

At minimum, a shared catch.

The judges are still reviewing the footage.

Corbin, meanwhile, declined to comment on the dispute. He was unavailable for interviews because he was skipping across the dock screaming that he caught a fish.

That brought the score to 1-1.

You could see it in Kaysen’s eyes. He knew the lead was gone. The younger competitor had pulled even, and suddenly this was not just a casual afternoon by the lake.

This was a brother showdown.

Cassie, who had been preparing to launch Kylie and Addy in the kayak division, abandoned that operation immediately and came running in for pictures. A strong move from the family media team.

Then came the moment that shook the dock.

Corbin hooked into another one.

Two to one.

The crowd went wild.

The crowd was mostly Corbin.

After taking the lead, he made the bold strategic decision to stop focusing on fishing and begin an extended celebration routine. There was more skipping. More yelling. A general lack of concern for maintaining momentum.

Some analysts questioned the move.

Others respected the confidence.

Kaysen, now trailing by one, went to the bench.

And by the bench, I mean he grabbed the two-eyed monster truck fishing pole.

This was a clear equipment adjustment.

His current setup had failed to produce the comeback fish, so naturally the problem had to be the lure. Or the pole. Or the fact that the other pole had two eyes and a monster truck on it.

That is just basic fishing science.

He gave it everything he had.

But the lake did not cooperate.

Meanwhile, Corbin continued to show surprising improvement in the casting department. His form was improving. His confidence was growing. His hook awareness remained a concern.

At one point, he hooked me.

Not badly.

Just the shirt.

But it was close enough that I briefly understood what bait must feel like.

That is the part they do not show you in the professional tournaments. Nobody talks about the father standing on the dock managing lines, fish, pictures, hooks, emotional disputes, and the possibility of becoming part of the tackle.

But that was my role.

Tournament official.

Safety officer.

Fish remover.

Appeals judge.

Human backstop.

By the end of regulation, the final score stood at Corbin 2, Kaysen 1.

There may be an asterisk depending on how the reel-in assistance claim is handled.

But for now, the record shows that one little boy caught his first fish, then caught another, and celebrated both with the kind of joy that makes everyone else stop what they are doing.

His older brother caught one too, started strong, fought hard, questioned the scoring, changed equipment, and learned the painful lesson that sometimes the fish do not care about your comeback story.

The fish were small.

The scoreboard was not.

And then came the biggest development of the day.

Both boys asked if we could go again tomorrow.

So we are.

That is the reel win.

I couldn’t resist.

Not the final count.

Not the size of the fish.

Not even the first fish, though that one will matter for a long time.

The win was that the dock did what it was supposed to do. It gave them just enough success, just enough chaos, and just enough unfinished business to make them want to come back.

Then we got home, and they still were not done.

They wanted to practice casting in the yard, so I took the lures off and put on a couple old ones with the hooks clipped off.

There they were, standing in the grass, casting like the tournament had followed us home.

No lake.

No dock.

No fish.

Just two boys, a couple fishing poles, and tomorrow already on the schedule.

Final results from the Dockside Classic:

Corbin: two fish, one first catch, multiple celebration laps.

Kaysen: one fish, one pending appeal, strong equipment adjustment.

Cassie: one missed paddle board celebration, one successful sprint from kayak duty.

Dad: zero fish, one shirt hooked, full heart.

And somehow, that feels like a pretty good day on the water.

Official standings will be reviewed overnight.

A rematch has already been scheduled.

Kaysen will be looking for redemption.

Corbin will be defending the title.

Dad will be wearing a shirt he is willing to lose.

Stay tuned for tomorrow’s results from the Dockside Classic.
