The Bus Wasn’t the Mission: What One Missed School Bus Taught Me About Intentional Fatherhood

I overslept, missed the bus timeline, and expected the morning to turn into chaos. Normally, it would have. But instead of rushing everyone out the door, I slowed down, let the bus go, grabbed donuts, and drove the kids to school. What happened next reminded me of something important: sometimes…

At 8:15 this morning, my internal EOD team leader informed me we were experiencing catastrophic timeline failure.

The kids’ bus comes around 8:30 and I just woke up—Not ideal to say the least.

And before anybody says it—yes, I know alarms exist.

This also isn’t my first time accidentally stress-testing the flexibility of the East Lyme school transportation system.

Normally, mornings like this go one of two ways.

I come downstairs already mentally behind.

  • Everybody move.
  • Shoes.
  • Backpacks.

Why are we still standing here?

Kylie, my oldest, can’t find her socks.

Kaysen forgot his water bottle.

Corbin, my youngest, is somehow trying to leave the house with one shoe on and no clear plan for fixing the situation.

Real calm leadership stuff.

If I’m being honest, mornings like that usually feel less like parenting and more like trying to evacuate civilians during a rapidly deteriorating situation.

And unfortunately, everybody feels it.

  • The kids feel it.
  • Cassie feels it.
  • I feel it.

By the time everyone leaves, it feels like the day already started wrong.

But this morning felt different.

Cassie had worked all night and wouldn’t be getting home until around the same time the bus came.

I was standing there realizing we had maybe fifteen minutes until the wheels completely came off when I stopped and asked myself something simple:

"What’s actually the mission here?"

And for once, the answer wasn’t: Recover the timeline.

Because if everybody gets out the door stressed, crying, frustrated, and emotionally exhausted, Did we actually win? Or did we just make the bus?

That question hit me harder than I expected.

So instead of coming downstairs like a drill sergeant trying to salvage a failed operation, I made a decision that honestly made my high-stakes brain uncomfortable:

We let the bus go.

I told the kids I’d drive them to school.

And because leadership sometimes requires tactical bribery, I promised Dunkin donuts on the way.

Shockingly, morale improved immediately.

  • No chaos.
  • No yelling.
  • No tears.

Nobody dragging a backpack to the bus stop while putting on shoes at the same time.

Just… calm.

  • We slowed down.
  • Everybody got ready.
  • We got donuts.

And I dropped them off at school around 9:15.

Honestly?

Not that much later than the bus would’ve gotten there anyway.

But that’s not the part that stayed with me.

Before they went into school, my kids hugged me, and something about it felt different.

Normally, it feels more like:

“We should hug dad before school.”

Today felt like:

“I want to hug dad before school.”

That one got me.

Because if I’m being honest, I think one of my biggest fears as a dad is becoming someone my

family experiences as pressure.

The guy who means well but always feels rushed.

The guy trying so hard to lead that he accidentally turns ordinary moments into problems that need

solving.

And I think a lot of that comes from the life I lived before this.

Military.

Emergency services.

Environments where urgency matters.

Where timelines matter.

Where being calm under pressure actually means something.

You learn to move fast.

Solve problems.

Control outcomes.

And honestly, those things can make you pretty competent.

But what nobody talks about is how that mindset quietly follows you home.

Suddenly bedtime feels urgent.

Baseball games feel stressful.

A missed shoe becomes a crisis.

A normal school morning starts feeling like failure because the timeline slipped.

The weird thing?

It doesn’t come from not caring.

It comes from caring too much.

Wanting to provide.

Wanting to lead.

Wanting things to go well.

But somewhere along the way, I think I started confusing control with presence.

This morning reminded me they’re not the same thing.

Because the bus wasn’t actually the mission.

The people were.

The mission was protecting the tone of the house.

The mission was making sure Cassie didn’t come home from a night shift to chaos.

The mission was sending my kids into school feeling calm instead of rushed.

The mission was being the kind of dad my kids actually wanted to hug before they left.

And the funny thing?

The morning that should’ve gone badly because I overslept ended up being one of the better

mornings we’ve had in a while.

That feels like a pretty good metaphor for what River Bend Outdoors is becoming.

This isn’t really a website about hiking.

Or gear.

Or pretending I have life figured out.

It’s about slowing down long enough to notice what actually matters.

It’s about becoming more intentional.

Learning how to lead better at home.

Breaking autopilot.

Trying to become more present as a husband and father.

Mostly, it’s me processing out loud.

Trying things.

Getting some reps right.

Missing others.

Then trying again.

So tomorrow morning, I’m not trying to be perfect.

I’m just trying to remember something simple before urgency takes over:

Slow Down. Be Present. Live Intentionally.

If any part of this sounded familiar, you’re probably not alone.

Maybe your version isn’t missing the bus.

Maybe it’s bedtime chaos.

Baseball practice.

Walking through the front door already stressed.

Rushing through dinner.

Trying so hard to hold everything together that you accidentally miss the people standing right in

front of you.

I’m still figuring this out too.

But if this hit home, I’d genuinely love to hear about it.

What’s one ordinary moment where you’re trying to show up differently?
