When Hunting Season Changes a Hike: Lyme's Closures and Sharing Connecticut's Woods Safely

By Cody Spencer

Ten Lyme preserves are closing on weekdays for firearm deer season, while hiking and hunting can coexist at nearby Nehantic State Forest. Here's how to know the difference and stay safe.

On November 10, the Town of Lyme announced something that could change a perfectly ordinary walk in the woods: ten local preserves would be closed to nonhunters on weekdays during the upcoming firearm deer hunting season.

From November 18 through December 22, 2026, the restrictions apply Monday through Friday at Beebe, Chestnut Hill, Eno, Hilles, Jewett, Johnston, Mount Archer Woods, Pickwick's, Plimpton, and Slawson preserves. Only hunters with the town's authorization may use those preserves on the restricted days. On Saturdays and Sundays, the preserves reopen to other visitors, and hunting under the program is prohibited there.

That sounds straightforward enough—until you notice the town's additional warning: hunting can still take place on adjoining public and private lands, including on weekends. A trail reopening doesn't mean the entire surrounding area has become a hunting-free zone.

For someone planning a hike, that distinction matters more than simply identifying whether a trail is open.

Same season, different rules

Consider nearby Nehantic State Forest. Connecticut's Department of Energy and Environmental Protection (DEEP) lists both hiking and hunting among its permitted activities. Hunting is limited to designated areas and subject to season-specific rules, but hikers generally aren't excluded from the entire forest simply because a hunting season is open. Visitors still need to check current area maps and posted notices.

Lyme's participating preserves take a different approach during firearm deer season: they separate permitted hunters from other visitors by day of the week.

Neither arrangement means hunters are automatically dangerous or hikers are unwelcome. The land managers have established different access rules for different properties. Those rules—not a broad assumption about hunting season—determine whether you can use a particular preserve or trail.

There is another distinction that gets lost easily. Conserved land isn't necessarily unrestricted public land. Some conservation properties are publicly owned, some belong to land trusts, and some remain private with conservation protections. Their owners and managers may allow different activities at different times. Before planning a visit, find out who manages the land and what activities are allowed—not just where the trail begins. That's the difference between a useful access guide and a trailhead pin on a map.

Hunting has a place in wildlife management

The Lyme Land Trust includes regulated deer and turkey hunting in its conservation work. When too many deer browse an area, young trees and other vegetation can struggle to recover; regulated hunting is one way land managers can respond. It won't produce the same result everywhere, but it helps explain why hunting and conservation can go hand in hand. Hikers, anglers, birdwatchers, and families still deserve clear information about when and where they can visit.

For hunters, one rule should never get lost in the conversation about orange clothing and shared trails: positively identify the target and know what is beyond it before taking a shot.

That means checking the direction of fire and what's behind the animal—not merely recognizing the animal itself. If another person, a trail, or the background makes the shot uncertain, don't take it.

Hunters also need to know property boundaries, current hunting permissions, the legal firearms and seasons for that area, and any additional land-manager restrictions. The obligation to make a safe shot remains with the person using the firearm. It does not transfer to a hiker because that hiker wore muted colors instead of fluorescent orange.

Connecticut generally requires hunters to wear at least 400 square inches of fluorescent orange above the waist, visible from all sides, during September through February, though the regulation has specific exceptions for certain hunting methods and situations. Hunters should consult the current DEEP guide rather than assuming every season has the same clothing rule.

The choices hikers can make before leaving home

Hikers aren't generally subject to a statewide fluorescent-orange clothing requirement, but DEEP recommends wearing conspicuous clothing where hunting may occur. Fluorescent orange is a practical choice because it's recognizable and visible in the woods. An orange hat, jacket, or backpack cover can help, especially if a pack obscures clothing on your back. DEEP also recommends making dogs visible with bright orange vests.

Start with the map, then check the property rules. Open Connecticut DEEP's interactive public hunting areas map to see state-managed and other DEEP-listed hunting areas. For Lyme's ten preserves, also read the town's current closure notice and the Lyme Land Trust hunting schedule. Finally, follow signs at trailheads and property boundaries. A map of hunting areas is not a live record of every local closure, and it does not override posted restrictions.

The state map isn't a complete inventory of hunting on every neighboring private parcel. That's particularly relevant around Lyme's preserves, where the town specifically warns that hunting may occur on adjacent land.

On an open trail where hunting is permitted, staying on marked routes, keeping dogs under control, and making yourself visible are sensible precautions. DEEP advises people who encounter a hunter to call out to identify their location. Don't assume they've seen you until you've made your presence known.

A trail can be open while hunting is in progress

This is the practical difference between Lyme and Nehantic that visitors need to understand.

At the ten named Lyme preserves, weekday access for nonhunters is restricted November 18–December 22, 2026. Weekend visitors may enter, but should remember that nearby hunting activity isn't necessarily suspended. At Nehantic, hiking and hunting are both recognized uses, so visitors should look up the permitted hunting areas and current notices rather than assuming the forest is closed—or that it's free of hunters.

There are plenty of ways to enjoy the same patch of woods. Some people come to hunt; others come to walk, fish, photograph wildlife, or simply spend an afternoon outdoors. Knowing the property rules and respecting other users makes those experiences easier to share.

Understanding a place means understanding more than how to get there. It means understanding why it's there, how it's managed, and our responsibilities when using it.
