What Do Those Colored Marks on Trees Mean? An East Lyme Trail Blaze Guide

By Cody

Blue, red, white, or two colors together—East Lyme's trail markers tell more than one story. Here's how to read them without assuming every park follows the same rules.

A colored mark on a tree looks like a simple thing. Follow the blue marks and you're on the blue trail, right?

Usually, that's a good start. But then you reach a junction with three colors, a white stripe paired with red, or a little numbered or lettered marker attached to a tree. Is one of those telling you to turn? Does another mean you're near an exit? And would the same combination mean the same thing at a different park?

Around East Lyme, the answer depends on which trail system you're using.

First, what is a blaze?

A blaze is a visible mark—often paint or a mounted marker—used to help hikers follow a trail. Its color may identify the route. Its shape or arrangement may add information about a turn, connection, or ending.

But there's no single set of meanings for every trail in Connecticut. Even within East Lyme, a nature preserve and a state park can use different combinations. The useful habit is to learn the markings for the place you're actually visiting rather than assume a familiar color means the same thing everywhere.

Fallen leaves can make the tread hard to distinguish, while the mounted trail marker remains visible.

Why we're using Oswegatchie Hills

Oswegatchie Hills Nature Preserve in East Lyme is an especially useful place to understand the difference. The Friends of Oswegatchie Hills provide a detailed trail map, describe the trails and their entrances, and explain some of the markers you'll encounter. That's much better for learning than guessing from a photograph of an unfamiliar tree.

The preserve's map shows several color-coded routes. The Friends' visitor guidance identifies the main public approach through Veterans Memorial Park and describes the southern entrance to the Red and Blue Trails.

Two directional arrows appear beside lettered junction markers on the same tree at Oswegatchie Hills.

The first important distinction is between a trail color and a location marker. At Oswegatchie, small red metal discs with letters mark intersections. Those letters also appear on the trail map, helping visitors work out which junction they've reached. The Friends say they can also help emergency personnel identify a location. A red disc with a letter isn't necessarily telling you to take the Red Trail; it's identifying where you are.

The second distinction is between a trail and an exit route. At Oswegatchie Hills, some markings that pair white with green, red, or blue identify routes toward alternate exits. But a white combination does not always mean an exit. The Red/White connecting trail described below is one example of a two-color marking used for a particular route. Check the preserve's current map and posted signs rather than assuming what a color combination means.

There's an important catch: a marked exit isn't automatically a public entrance or exit today. In their third- and fourth-quarter 2025 newsletter, the Friends reported that the Blue/White entrance at Chapman Woods became private and residents-only. Always check current access information, not just the color of a marker.

This arrow gives a visible direction cue. Its meaning is local to the trail system and should be checked against the preserve map.

When two colors appear together

Two colors on one marker don't necessarily mean two separate trails are sharing the same stretch of path.

At Oswegatchie, the Friends reported opening a Red/White-blazed connecting trail from the Veterans Memorial Park ballfield area to the Red Loop in 2025. They also described a new purple loop whose purple blazes have a reflective fluorescent-orange band across the middle for visibility.

In those cases, the combination is part of a particular route's documented marking. It doesn't mean that every red-and-white marker in Connecticut has the same meaning—or that the orange band identifies a separate orange route.

This is why a park's own legend is so valuable: it tells you what its colors and symbols are intended to communicate.

Rocky Neck shows how different a local map can be

Just a short distance away, Rocky Neck State Park's official trail map presents a different-looking network. The Connecticut Department of Energy and Environmental Protection's March 5, 2026 map has 12 trail-color categories, including blue, red, white, yellow, and combinations such as Blue/Red, Blue/White, and Red/Yellow.

Those combinations are shown as alternating colors in the map's legend. That's useful for following a mapped route and for recognizing when your intended route changes. But the legend alone does not prove exactly how every corresponding blaze is painted or mounted in the woods. That requires photographs or a specific trail-marking description.

The lesson isn't to memorize all 12 Rocky Neck categories. It's to find the color or combination for the trail you're taking and check the map again whenever routes meet.

What about two blazes stacked together?

Sometimes a marking communicates direction, not just identity. The Connecticut Forest & Park Association's blaze guide shows a common painted-rectangle system used on trails it maintains: one vertical rectangle means continue, two vertically offset rectangles signal a turn toward the upper mark, and two horizontal rectangles mark the end of a trail.

Those examples are useful to recognize. They are CFPA's conventions, though—not proof that every Oswegatchie or Rocky Neck marker uses the same shapes or turn arrangements.

Example of offset painted blazes indicating a left turn; photographed outside Oswegatchie Hills.

What to do at a confusing junction

Before setting out, identify your intended trail color on the location's own map. If there's a lettered junction or another distinct reference point, note it. When trails meet, check which marked route continues, and compare that with the map and the direction you arrived from. Don't treat the most obvious path as automatically the correct one.

An orange blaze helps identify the marked trail when fallen leaves make the footpath difficult to follow.

Wider views show the marker in relation to the route rather than only as an isolated symbol.

If you can't confidently match the markings to your map, stop somewhere safe and reassess. Retracing your known route to the last confirmed marker is usually more sensible than guessing at an unmarked shortcut. At Oswegatchie, the Friends specifically warn that unmarked paths may not appear on their map and ask visitors to stay on marked trails.

The next time you notice a colored rectangle, a two-color marker, or a letter on a tree, ask two separate questions: What trail does this identify? And what, if anything, is it telling me to do here?

Often, knowing the difference is the whole trick.
