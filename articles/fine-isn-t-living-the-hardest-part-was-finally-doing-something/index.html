The Hardest Part Was Finally Doing Something

A 45-minute VA mental health appointment didn’t magically fix anything, but it helped me realize something important: the hardest part of change is finally doing something. A reflection on fatherhood, marriage, mental health, and learning that “fine” isn’t really living.

For a long time, I told myself I was fine.

Not great. Not particularly happy. But fine.

And if I’m being honest, I think a lot of husbands, fathers, veterans, and first responders quietly live in that same place.

We go to work. We provide. We coach baseball. We mow the lawn. We fix things around the house. We survive bedtime and try to keep the wheels from falling off. Life gets loud, stressful, and busy, and somewhere along the way, “fine” quietly becomes the standard.

You stop asking yourself:

“Am I actually happy?”

And start telling yourself:

“Things will get better when the kids are older.”

“Life will calm down when work slows up.”

“I just need a little more time to myself.”

That was me.

The problem is that life never really slows down.

Stress just changes shape.

The kids don’t stop needing you—they just need you differently. Marriage still takes effort. Your mental health doesn’t magically improve because the calendar flips.

At some point, waiting quietly turns into years.

And honestly?

I think I was in denial.

Not because things were good.

Because they weren’t bad enough.

At least, that’s what I told myself.

In my mind, therapy was for people who couldn’t function day to day. People who had been blown up, shot at, or seen the worst of the worst.

Not the guy who trained for war and mostly stayed on base in Iraq.

Not the guy who still went to work.

Still paid the bills.

Still showed up.

I knew things weren’t okay.

But I didn’t think they were bad enough to justify help.

So, I kicked the can down the road.

What Finally Changed

The thing that finally changed wasn’t some dramatic breakdown.

It wasn’t one huge fight.

It wasn’t some moment where life completely fell apart.

Honestly?

One day I just started seeing myself differently.

Or maybe more accurately:

I started seeing myself through someone else’s eyes.

For years, I had measured myself against my own standards.

I provide.

I’m responsible.

I show up.

I handle what needs handling.

By those standards?

I was doing fine.

But then something shifted.

I stopped measuring myself against my standards and started measuring myself against my family’s.

What did my wife actually experience when she experienced me?

What did my kids feel around me?

Was Dad calm?

Present?

Patient?

Connected?

Or was Dad stressed?

Rushed?

Emotionally unavailable?

Trying to get through one thing so he could get to the next?

That realization humbled me fast.

Because the more honest I got, the more I realized how misaligned our standards had become.

I thought I was succeeding because I was handling responsibilities.

Meanwhile, the people I loved most were quietly experiencing a version of me I didn’t even like.

And the hardest truth?

The further I pushed toward my goals of honestly doing nothing…

The further away I drifted from my family.

Scheduling the Appointment Was Harder Than the Appointment

Right after that perspective shift, I scheduled a VA mental health appointment.

And honestly?

That alone felt like progress.

But if I’m being real, the appointment took forever to actually get scheduled.

Long enough that I probably would’ve kicked the can down the road again.

My wife was starting to worry I’d let it quietly fall by the wayside like usual.

And if I’m honest?

She probably had good reason to think that.

Right as I was about to follow up, the appointment finally showed up.

Perfect timing.

Because I needed accountability.

I think a lot of guys do.

Therapy Wasn’t What I Thought It Was

When the appointment finally came around, I expected the usual.

“How are you?”

“What’s been going on?”

A one-hour conversation where I go through the motions and move on.

But this was different.

This was an orientation for an in-person intensive therapy program—one to two sessions a week for eight weeks.

And honestly?

That excited me.

Which surprised me.

Not because therapy sounded exciting.

But because for the first time, it felt real.

Structured.

Intentional.

Like there was an actual plan.

Not just:

“Talk about feelings and hope things improve.”

The program includes one-on-one therapy, but it may also include group sessions.

And weirdly enough, that part made me hopeful too.

Because if I’m being honest, part of me thinks I need that accountability.

It’s easier to blow off a virtual appointment because something “came up.”

Harder to skip something in person when people are waiting on you.

Honestly?

It feels familiar.

Like showing up to PT with your group.

Nobody really wants to go at first.

But everyone shows up because showing up matters.

And maybe that sounds weird, but part of me is also hopeful that maybe someone else there is struggling with some of the same things I am.

Maybe hearing another perspective helps me get out of my own head.

Maybe something I say helps someone else too.

Or maybe—if I’m being really honest—I’m hoping to find someone who gets it.

Because a lot of men don’t really have places to say:

“I’m struggling.”

Especially dads.

Especially veterans.

Especially first responders.

We’ll vent about stress.

Talk about sports.

Complain about the kids.

But saying:

“I think I’m emotionally disconnected.”

“I’m worried about my marriage.”

“I don’t know how to process emotions.”

That feels different.

What Changed After the First Step

The biggest thing that came out of that appointment wasn’t some breakthrough.

Nobody fixed me in 45 minutes.

I didn’t walk away suddenly healed.

What changed was this:

There was momentum.

Measurable momentum.

I’ve already been trying to become more intentional in my life.

Trying to slow down.

Trying to be more present.

Trying to stop surviving next to my family.

Now?

There’s a mental health plan too.

For the first time in a long time, things feel like they’re moving in the right direction instead of getting kicked down the road.

And honestly?

That feels exciting.

Because all I can do now is be a little better than I was yesterday.

That’s it.

Not perfect.

Not fixed.

Just better.

One day at a time.

Let's Be Real for a Second

If you take one thing away from this, let it be this:

Take a second and stop measuring yourself against your own standards.

Look at yourself through your wife’s eyes.

Through your kids’ eyes.

What do they actually experience when they experience you?

And no guys—be real for a second.

Not what you intend them to feel.

Not what you hope they feel.

What do they actually feel?

Do they feel calm around you?

Safe?

Important?

Connected?

Or do they feel rushed?

Like they’re interrupting something?

Like Dad is physically there but mentally somewhere else?

Because sometimes the hardest part of change isn’t therapy.

It’s finally being honest enough to admit something needs to change.

And after that?

The hardest part is already over.

You just have to take the first step.
