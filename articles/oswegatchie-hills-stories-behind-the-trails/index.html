Oswegatchie Hills: Trails, Ponds, and a Few Surprises

By Cody

Beyond the ballfields, rocky trails lead toward Clark Pond—a quiet stretch of water with a surprising history tied to Niantic’s fishing fleet.

The trails at Oswegatchie Hills take you from the ballfields into rocky woods, quiet water, and a landscape with stories that aren't immediately obvious.

The last time I hiked here with the boys, I put Kaysen in charge of navigation.

We stopped at a trail map attached to a tree, and I let him figure out where we were and where we could go next. Corbin had his own approach to the hike: if there was a rock nearby, he probably needed to step on it.

Eventually, we had to decide whether to keep going around the pond or head back. Kaysen considered the distance, looked at his little brother, and made his recommendation.

“Probably not because Corbin is s..l..o..w.”

He wasn't wrong. We turned around.

It's one of the things I like about a place such as Oswegatchie Hills. You can start with a route in mind, follow the trail markers, stop to see what's around you, and decide how much of the preserve you want to explore. Finishing a loop isn't a requirement.

Back in the woods on Veterans Day

On November 11, I had an unexpected little opening in my schedule between dropping Corbin off and the kids' Veterans Day school events. I was wearing ordinary shoes, not dressed for some epic hike, and figured I could spend that window walking and taking pictures of a place worth knowing better.

That was a separate trip from the walk with the boys. This time I paid attention to the kinds of things that help someone else have a good visit: the posted maps, the trail markers, and what the ground actually looks like once the leaves come down.

Woods, water, and more than one trail

Oswegatchie Hills Nature Preserve protects over 450 acres of wooded ridges, wetlands, ponds, and land overlooking the Niantic River in East Lyme. From the athletic fields at Veterans Memorial Park, the marked trails take you into a landscape that feels very different from the ballfields near the entrance.

There's plenty to look for along the way: rock outcrops, stretches of forest, changes in elevation, and places to stop and take in the water. Some parts of the trail network are fairly gentle; others are rocky and steep enough that you should look at the map before committing to a longer route.

The Friends of Oswegatchie Hills trail map marks different trail colors, intersection-location tags, and twelve points of interest. You don't need to visit all twelve. The map is just as useful for deciding where to turn around as it is for planning where to go next.

Clark Pond is a good place to start

If you're looking for a shorter introduction to the preserve, the Friends recommend the opening section of the Blue Trail, crossing the bridge near Clark Pond and continuing toward a pond overlook. You can return the same way rather than taking on a full loop.

That recommendation doesn't mean every step is smooth or suitable for a stroller. Trails can have roots, rocks, and wet or uneven footing, and conditions change with the weather. The stewards describe this as a less-steep option, not an accessible paved path.

Clark Pond also has a little local history worth knowing while you're there. According to the Friends, citing East Lyme Town Historian Elizabeth Hall Kuchta, ice harvested from the pond once helped Niantic fishermen preserve their catch. It's a small detail, but it changes how you look at a pond beside a hiking trail.

A few things hidden on the map

The pond isn't the only place with a story.

One of the map's marked destinations is an old granite quarry. The East Lyme Historical Society documents granite being taken from these hills in the 1800s for local construction, including a stone house near Smith Cove.

Another marker identifies the site of a former homestead. Archaeological work involving East Lyme High School students uncovered household objects and possible traces of a hearth, suggesting that people once lived there. The details of that home's history are not completely settled, and anything remaining at the site should be left undisturbed. Save the Sound has more about those discoveries.

Those aren't reasons to turn a hike into a history assignment. They're simply interesting things to have in the back of your mind as you explore.

And it's worth remembering that the woods are still here because residents and conservation groups worked to protect them. The preserve didn't just happen to stay undeveloped.

Before you go

The Town of East Lyme identifies the main entrance at Veterans Memorial Field, 10 Memorial Park Drive, Niantic, off Pennsylvania Avenue/Route 161. Look for the trail kiosk near the athletic fields.

The preserve's visitor guidance says trails are open from sunrise to sunset, dogs must be leashed, and visitors should stay on marked routes. Some trails have steep sections and rocky ledges. Restrooms are listed at neighboring Veterans Memorial Park, but check availability before relying on them.

Bring up the official trail map before heading in, pay attention to the marked intersections, and choose a route that makes sense for the time and people you've brought. The quarry, pond, and other points of interest don't all fall along one short walk.

I found printed trail maps available at the kiosk on November 11. They may not always be stocked, so I would still save the Friends' official trail map to my phone. Use the posted trailhead; don't assume every path shown near a neighborhood is an open public entrance.

And next time Kaysen is navigating, I'll be interested to see whether Corbin has gotten any faster.

Worth making time for

On the way back through Veterans Memorial Park, I kept thinking how much work had gone into making these trails welcoming: visible markers, a useful map, and a clear way to begin. It felt quiet on this visit. That's only my impression, not a count of how busy the preserve gets.

I still had the school events to get to. I hadn't walked every trail, or even worn my hiking boots, but I'd gotten outside, noticed a few things worth sharing, and enjoyed a bit of Veterans Day doing something I love. Sometimes that is more than enough.
