The Invitations Are Changing

What a county fair, a truck pull, and two minutes of violin noise showed me about entering the worlds of the people I love.

We do not have to share the same favorite part of the day to share the day together.

The first truck had barely started its run when I realized I was watching two childhoods at once.

Part of me was back in North Carolina, remembering the noise, the powerful trucks, the dirt thrown behind them, and the way the sled became heavier as it moved down the track.

The other part of me was watching my boys experience it for the first time.

Corbin stood on the bleachers after the first truck and barely sat down again. Kaysen and his friend laughed, shouted, and hyped each other up after every run.

They loved it.

Kylie did not.

Cassie did not really care for it either.

She was there because we were there. Because I wanted to go. Because it mattered to me.

I did not fully understand why that stayed with me until later.

Corbin was ready for every run.

The pull was the part of the fair I had been waiting for.

I found the North Stonington Agricultural Fair while researching local weekend events for River Bend Outdoors.

The schedule included livestock, rides, tractors, ox pulls, and a Southern New England Truck Pull. It looked a lot like the agricultural fairs I remembered growing up around in North Carolina.

That was enough for me.

We planned to arrive early, wander the grounds, let the kids ride a few rides, and make our way to the truck pull later in the afternoon.

It was the kind of family day I have been trying to create more intentionally—the kind that actually gets placed on the calendar instead of disappearing beneath yardwork, errands, and everything else that can consume a weekend.

Volunteer firefighters directed us into a dusty grass parking field. We walked downhill toward the fairgrounds, and somewhere near the covered livestock area, I caught the smell of hay.

It immediately felt familiar.

Once inside, our first priority was finding the bathrooms. That has become one of my standard rules when taking three kids anywhere: know where the bathroom is before somebody desperately needs it.

On the way, the kids climbed on tractors and utility vehicles. Cassie started looking for coffee. Kylie wanted to have a caricature drawn. The boys, Kaysen’s friend, and I stopped beneath the covered area to watch part of the ox pull.

They immediately started asking questions.

How much weight were the oxen pulling?

How did the competition work?

What counted as a successful pull?

I understood the general idea, but I did not know all the rules. That did not matter. For a while, we stood there watching and tried to figure it out together.

That became the rhythm of the day.

We never stayed for an entire event. We moved when somebody became curious about something else. The schedule gave us a general direction, but it never became a checklist.

The kids rode rides. Kylie had her caricature drawn. We looked at animals. We ate fair food. We walked through the dust, trying to keep track of tickets, water bottles, and six people experiencing the same fair in slightly different ways.

The smell of hay near the livestock area immediately felt familiar.

The truck pull was not everyone’s favorite part of the fair—and it did not need to be.

Around lunchtime, we returned to our minivan.

We had packed a cooler and left it in the vehicle so we could use the van as a base camp. The kids ate sandwiches in the air conditioning while I sat near the back in a camping chair, guarding the cooler from a pack of feral beasts who apparently had not eaten in almost three minutes.

We refilled water bottles, reapplied sunscreen, and gave everybody a chance to slow down. The boys threw a football between the parking rows while the adults ate.

It was not the most exciting part of the fair, but it may have been one of the reasons the rest of the day worked. We saved some money, avoided pushing everyone past their limits, and returned to the fairgrounds with enough energy to keep going.

Our minivan became a base camp—a place to eat, cool down, refill, and reset.

The truck pull was still the event I had been waiting for.

The boys’ excitement made it even better.

But afterward, I kept thinking about the people who had not been as excited as I was.

The hardest part of raising two boys who are a lot like me is how easily the center of gravity in our family can shift toward the things we enjoy.

Fishing.

Camping.

Trucks.

Exploring the woods.

Those activities come naturally to the three of us. I know how to plan them, how to talk about them, and how to build excitement around them.

Without intending to, I can make those things the default and leave Kylie and Cassie following behind us.

I can tell myself we are having a family adventure when, in reality, I have planned something primarily around what the boys and I want to do.

That realization does not apply only to my kids.

It applies to my marriage too.

Cassie showed up to that fair knowing it was not really her thing. She walked the grounds, helped manage the chaos, looked for coffee, and stayed present—not because she loved truck pulls or agricultural fairs, but because she loves us.

I am starting to notice how often she does that.

I am also starting to notice how easily I can treat it as normal instead of recognizing it as a gift.

Her willingness to step into my world should not always be the default.

I need to step into hers too.

That does not mean I should stop taking my children fishing or camping. I still want to share the things that shaped me. I want my boys to know how to read the water, build a fire, sleep outside, and appreciate the kind of quiet that can only be found away from a screen.

I want Kylie to have those experiences too.

But I am beginning to understand that sharing my world with my family is only half of the relationship.

When my children were little, almost every invitation came from me.

Come fishing.

Come camping.

Come walk this trail.

Come see this animal.

Come look at this truck.

As they get older, the invitations are changing.

They are beginning to ask me to enter their worlds.

That can be harder than it sounds.

The setting is not always the one I pictured. The activity may not interest me. I may not understand why something matters as much as it does to them.

But the activity is not always the point.

Kylie does not need to love a truck pull to want to share a day with me.

Cassie does not need to love a county fair to show up for our family.

And I do not need to love makeup to walk through Ulta with Kylie and listen while she explains what she likes.

I took her fishing. She can take me makeup shopping.

I took her camping. She can invite me into something she wants to share.

Cassie has spent years stepping into my world. I want to become more intentional about stepping into hers.

When Kylie is older, I do not want her to remember only that I showed her all of my favorite experiences.

I want her to remember that we shared ours.

When I look back on my marriage, I do not want to measure it by how often Cassie joined me in the things I loved.

I want to be able to name the places where I chose to enter her world too.

Sharing the day did not require everyone to love the same things.

There is another part of this that I want my boys to see.

I want them to love fishing. I want them to become capable outdoorsmen. But I also want them to grow into men who understand that loving someone does not mean continually convincing that person to enter their world.

Sometimes love means entering someone else’s.

Sometimes you have to go makeup shopping before you go fishing.

Sometimes you go to a fair you do not really care about because someone you love does.

I can tell my boys that, but they will probably learn more by watching whether I actually do it.

That may be as important as anything I teach them while holding a fishing rod.

Taking my mental health more seriously has helped me notice moments like this.

Therapy has not given me a perfect response to every situation. It has not made me endlessly patient or removed habits I have built over a lifetime.

It has helped me slow down enough to notice some of my assumptions.

That because someone does not love an activity, they do not want to share the experience.

That because Cassie is willing to come along, I do not need to bring the same willingness into the things she loves.

That because my boys’ interests resemble mine, those interests should naturally set the direction for the whole family.

Therapy has helped me recognize that my intentions and another person’s experience are not always the same.

More than anything, it has helped me pay closer attention.

A few days after the fair, Kylie received the violin she will be learning to play in middle school.

She asked me to watch while she tried it.

For about two minutes, she made noise with it before moving on to something else.

I watched the entire time.

It was not much of a performance, but that was never really what she was asking for.

She wanted me to share her excitement.

She wanted me to see her.

And she saw that I was watching.

I am beginning to understand that sometimes the invitation is that small.

So is the window to accept it.
