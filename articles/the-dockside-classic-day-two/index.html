The Dockside Classic: Day Two

The brothers return to the lake for a fishing rematch—with new scoring rules and plenty of family debate.

Ladies and gentlemen, welcome back to the Dockside Classic.

After yesterday’s controversial finish, a rematch was inevitable.

The competitors returned to the lake with confidence, unfinished business, and a scoring system that had already become more complicated than anyone intended.

Before a single line hit the water, we had our first accusation.

I told the kids the tournament would officially start after I took a picture of them.

Simple enough.

Then Cassie told me to get in the picture too, so she took one of the four of us.

After that, she wanted one of just the kids.

So I left.

Not dramatically.

Not secretly.

I just started walking toward the dock because, as previously stated, the tournament started after the picture.

I had not even put a line in the water yet when they came running behind me yelling that I was cheating.

Apparently walking toward the dock counts as a head start.

No cast had been made.

No fish had been caught.

No competitive advantage had been gained, unless you count understanding the rules as they were explained.

And just like that, Day Two was underway.

The next major development came from the rules committee.

By rules committee, I mean Cassie.

Apparently she read the Day One article, because she announced that if you help reel in a fish, you get half a point.

This was a major ruling.

It meant Kaysen’s legal team had been at least partially successful.

Under the updated scoring system, he had two points.

Corbin had two points.

The boys were tied.

The scoreboard had been corrected.

The committee had spoken.

The Dockside Classic was now operating under official half-point rules, which is exactly how all serious family fishing tournaments eventually fall apart.

Corbin came into Day Two as a defending champion, but his patience did not make the trip with him.

He cast about five times.

No fish.

That was enough.

He stepped away from competition for what officials are calling a nutrition and morale reset.

There was a snack.

Then he and Kaysen went inside to play ping pong.

I may have stayed behind and tried casting a few more times before catching up with them. Which was not cheating, that was veteran tournament awareness.

after a few casts of no luck, i figured it was time to go see what everybody else was ip to. By the time I made it inside to the rec cabin, Kaysen was feeling like a ping pong boss since he had been playing against Corbin who was swinging his paddle like a wild man.

Kaysen was playing something that looked like ping pong.

Corbin was playing a sport loosely inspired by ping pong, but mostly governed by volume, movement, and rules he appeared to be inventing as he went.

While that was happening, Cassie and Kylie were in the craft room making anklets.

So by the middle of Day Two, the Dockside Classic had expanded to include fishing, kayaking, ping pong, snacks, and jewelry production.

At that point we were less of a family at the lake and more of a small summer camp with a questionable chain of command.

Outside, we had our usual setup.

Picnic table.

Snacks.

The trusty wagon.

And the Goodwill cooler.

That cooler deserves its own recognition.

I bought it for about twenty dollars, and somehow it has become one of the best coolers we own. There are expensive coolers out there with brand names, warranties, and people who use them as personality traits.

Then there is our Goodwill cooler, showing up every time and quietly doing its job.

Late in the day, I finally hooked into my first fish.

It made it all the way to the dock.

It touched the dock.

Dock contact was established.

Then it flopped back into the water before Kaysen could get the picture.

I stood there in SHOCK.

My first fish of the day was gone.

No picture.

No proof.

Just me on the dock, robbed by a bluegill and poor camera timing.

Naturally, I appealed to the nearest witnesses.

Cassie was over in the sand playing with Corbin, so I yelled across the dock.

“Did you see my fish?”

She looked over and said no.

Then I asked the lifeguards.

All I got were smiles and heads shaking.

No.

No one saw it.

No witnesses.

No photo.

No justice.

They were playing me.

And honestly, I think I would have been more disappointed if they had not taken the opportunity.

That might be one of my favorite parts of the whole day.

We have been there enough now that I can yell across the dock asking if anyone saw my disputed fish, and the lifeguards are comfortable enough to joke around and deny the whole thing.

A few visits in, and we already have lake friends willing to participate in a cover-up.

That feels like progress.

But now I had a problem.

I could not let my first fish of the day exist only as an unverified dock-contact incident.

So I had to catch another one.

Not wanted to.

Had to.

For the integrity of the tournament.

Eventually, I did get one with photographic evidence.

A confirmed fish.

A documented fish.

A fish that could be entered into the official record without relying on corrupt witnesses, slow photography, or the honor system.

By my count, I finished with three.

Technically.

One remains under review, but I know what happened. The dock knows what happened. The fish knows what happened.

Unfortunately, the fish has not responded to requests for comment.

Later, Kylie and I were getting ready to take a kayak out when one of the lifeguards started giving us the rules.

Then she stopped herself.

“Wait, haven’t you guys been here the last like three days?”

I said, “Yup. We live just down the road, so we’ll be here often.”

And that is when I realized we may have accidentally become regulars.

At this rate, I give it about two more visits before Corbin is standing with the lifeguards telling new families the rules and handing out life jackets.

He does the same thing at baseball.

He does not really enter a crowd.

He just wiggles himself into it until everyone accepts that he belongs there.

The lake is small enough for that to work.

That is part of why I like it.

It is not so big that the kids disappear and we spend the whole day doing headcounts like stressed-out camp counselors. Between me, Cassie, and our new lifeguard friends, we have eyes pretty much everywhere.

The kids can move around a little.

They can fish.

They can play ping pong.

They can make anklets.

They can go out on kayaks.

They can make snacks disappear.

They can bounce from one thing to the next without the whole day falling apart.

At one point, Kaysen and Kylie went off to play tetherball.

It started as a normal game and eventually became what most sibling games become: trying to hit each other with the ball.

A natural progression.

By the end of regulation, the standings were still under review.

Kaysen had two.

Corbin had two.

I had three, technically.

Cassie had zero.

Kylie had zero.

The lifeguards had one successful denial of a valid fish claim.

Then the weather moved in.

We were just about ready to leave when the storm rolled across the lake.

The rain did not stay long, but it wanted to make sure we knew it was there.

It came down hard.

Cassie and I sat under the overhang on the dock.

The kids ran around in the rain.

No one was fishing anymore.

No one was keeping score.

No one was arguing about half-points, dock contact, slow photography, or whether the lifeguards were part of a larger conspiracy.

For a few minutes, the Dockside Classic became a rain delay with children who refused to acknowledge the delay.

And that might have been the best part of Day Two.

The first day was about the boys catching fish and asking to come back.

Day Two was about realizing this place might become part of us.

Not in some big dramatic way.

Just in the normal way places do when you keep showing up.

The lifeguards start recognizing you.

The kids know where the games are.

The wagon gets packed without much discussion.

The Goodwill cooler earns another day of service.

Someone makes an anklet.

Someone claims a fish that no one will verify.

Someone runs through the rain instead of hiding from it.

And before you know it, the lake is not just somewhere you went.

It is somewhere your family goes.

Official Day Two results:

Kaysen: two points, solid ping pong performance, continued awareness of the scoreboard.

Corbin: two points, one snack-based recovery plan, strong ping pong chaos, and future lifeguard potential.

Kylie: zero fish, one kayak outing, craft room production, and tetherball escalation.

Cassie: zero fish, one scoring rule change, one suspicious denial of my disputed catch.

Dad: three fish, technically, one dock-contact scandal, one confirmed photo, and an ongoing grievance with the witness committee.

Goodwill cooler: undefeated.

Lifeguards: friendly, helpful, and clearly not above messing with me.

Weather: brief, loud, and effective.

Final ruling:

The Dockside Classic remains active.

The standings are under review.

And we are probably going back again.
